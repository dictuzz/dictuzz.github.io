34 people. 10 divisions. One pandemic.

Chairman, Student Council · Saint Mary 2 High School, Bandung · Nov 2021 – Nov 2022

The council inherited a communication structure built for hallways. Then COVID shut the school. Ten divisions that used to coordinate face-to-face now ran through group chats and video calls. Half the members had never met each other in person.

The instinct was to centralize — run everything through the chair, reduce variables. We did the opposite. Each division head became autonomous over their own work, with one rule: a written one-paragraph update, weekly, on time. No meetings unless someone asked for one.

Three divisions outperformed the previous year's entire council output. Two stalled — their leads treated autonomy as permission to disappear. The fix was ugly: reassign members mid-term, fold one division into another. The promotional campaign to 12 feeder schools — which produced a viral TikTok — came from the division I almost shut down. It was rescued, not planned.

Trust without structure is abandonment. My job was never to lead 34 people. It was to build 10 leaders and intervene when one of them couldn't lead yet.

12 hours. Every week. That's how much time Finance lost to a process no one had questioned.

Management Trainee · Home Digital Indonesia · Jul 2024 – Sep 2025

The Finance team ran weekly reports by copying data between three spreadsheets, reformatting columns, and emailing summaries to five people. Nobody thought it was broken. It was just how reporting works here.

I didn't ask for permission to change it. I built the macro on my own laptop, tested it against three weeks of manual reports, and sent the comparison to my supervisor: same output, six minutes instead of twelve hours.

The macro shipped. Finance adopted it. Then Sales asked for one. Then Operations. The work expanded into a cross-team SOP standardization project — something no one had planned or requested. It happened because solving one broken process exposed three more.

The uncomfortable part: automating someone's daily task means telling them the thing they've been doing for months was unnecessary. Not everyone took that well.

Automation isn't a technical problem. It's a political one. The code takes an afternoon. The conversation takes weeks.

1,000 people in the seats. No documented transition procedure. Showtime approaching.

Stage Crew · Ultimate Event Organizer · Jun 2022 – Aug 2022

Event production looks like creativity from the audience side. From backstage, it's logistics. Which act transitions in what order. Where cables run so no one trips. Who has authority to call a hold if something breaks. These aren't artistic decisions. They're operational ones, and they have to be made before anything creative can happen.

The existing process was tribal knowledge — each crew member understood their piece but nobody had documented the sequence. Transitions ran on instinct, which meant they ran on luck.

I wrote the runsheet. Not because anyone asked — because a transition gap left 1,000 people watching dead air. After the runsheet, transitions tightened to under 90 seconds. The solution wasn't clever. It was a document.

Most problems that look like talent problems are actually documentation problems.
