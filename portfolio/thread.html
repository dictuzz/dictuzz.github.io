Every role I've held has been the same job: walk into disorder, find the structure it's missing, and leave something that runs without me.

Structure

The first real system I built was an acolyte schedule for a parish. It was a spreadsheet. Before that, the assignments were made by phone calls the week before each mass, and someone always got missed.

At Home Digital Indonesia, the scale changed but the pattern didn't. Three teams — Finance, Sales, Operations — had their own reporting formats, their own processes, their own definitions of what "done" meant. I wrote the SOPs. I built the macros. I ran the feedback surveys. The job title said Management Trainee. The actual job was: make this company's operations legible.

I study data science because structure is what I believe in. Data without structure is noise. Teams without structure are crowds. I'm not interested in data for its own sake — I'm interested in what happens when you organize it correctly.

Stage

I've been an MC since high school. Not the hype kind — the kind that keeps a 400-person event on tempo. Reading a room is the same skill as reading a dataset: you're looking for the pattern, the outlier, the moment something shifts.

Stage crew taught me that what the audience sees is 10% of the work. Backstage is where the real coordination happens — cable runs, transition timing, contingency plans for equipment that hasn't been tested. I wrote the first runsheet my production team had ever used. It was a document, not a performance.

In 2023, I spoke to journalism and communications students at Universitas Parahyangan. The preparation took longer than the talk. The content part was easy. Learning to hold silence in front of 60 people — that took practice.

That same year, an Instagram Reel I made for a German-language competition won first place at Goethe-Institut Bandung. Back in 2019, a short film I directed placed first at a diocesan competition. I don't call myself a filmmaker. But I've spent enough time thinking about how to hold an audience's attention across media.

Service

I've spent most of my volunteer life in Catholic organizations. Vice Chairman of the youth organization, Vice Chief Acolyte, council leadership in parish contexts. These roles don't go on most portfolios. I include them because they taught me something corporate roles didn't: how to lead people who can leave at any time.

Volunteers don't report to you. They chose to be there, and they can choose to stop. Managing 11 volunteers for a 400-person worship event is harder than managing 34 paid council members. You can't rely on obligation. You rely on clarity — clear roles, clear expectations, clear gratitude.

Vertical rescue is the most literal version of service I've done. Equipment maintenance, path climbing, training support. There's no audience. There's no metric. Someone might need help on a mountain, and when they do, the gear has to work.
